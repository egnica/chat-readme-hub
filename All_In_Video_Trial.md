# ALL IN: Nick's Six-Week Video Production Trial

November 2–December 13, 2026 • Final review Monday, December 14

## The decision

Make paid video production and editing the primary career objective. Run a focused YouTube series as a creative and audience-building experiment alongside it. Do not make replacing income through YouTube advertising a six-week requirement.

This is a practical course proposal, not a promise of employment, revenue, or channel growth. Live web research failed while this first edition was prepared on October 30. No workshop dates, course prices, creator case studies, or platform requirements were verified. The curriculum below is instructional judgment; the research backlog at the end remains open.

The trial tests three questions:

1. Can Nick repeatedly finish good work within realistic time limits?
2. Which parts of producing, shooting, and editing does he want to do for money?
3. Does focused work plus targeted outreach generate credible evidence of demand?

## The production system

Every video passes through the same checkpoints:

1. **Brief:** intended viewer, one takeaway, reason to care, destination, deadline, maximum length, and effort cap.
2. **Promise:** three honest title options, one thumbnail sketch, and a two-sentence opening before shooting. For business work, define the audience and desired action instead of chasing clicks.
3. **Preproduction:** a short outline or script, shot list, equipment check, location and sound check, permissions as applicable, and a fallback if the planned shoot fails.
4. **Production:** record a test, listen on headphones, check focus and exposure, then capture the essential performance and supporting shots.
5. **Postproduction:** assemble the argument, remove repetition, tighten the opening, then address sound, color, graphics, captions, and export. Decorative effects come after a clear story.
6. **Quality control:** watch the exported file from beginning to end. Check intelligible speech, accurate names and captions, visual consistency, claims, rights, and destination links.
7. **Delivery and review:** publish or deliver, log actual hours, get feedback, and choose one improvement for the next piece.

Never treat a good camera image as a substitute for a reason to watch. Never treat an interesting premise as a substitute for intelligible audio and dependable delivery.

### Week 1: finish and establish a baseline

**Monday, November 2 — four hours**

- 30 minutes: choose the exact Biff/Griff claim or a narrower movie topic. Write the brief.
- 45 minutes: draft three titles, thumbnail concept, and opening. State what the viewer will learn by the end.
- 45 minutes: build a short outline and list the footage/assets actually available. Remove anything that will require an open-ended search.
- 60 minutes: record three 20-second camera tests. Change one variable at a time, such as light direction, framing, or microphone position. Listen and compare.
- 60 minutes: revise the outline and prepare Tuesday's shot list. Include a fallback using talking head and original graphics.

**Tuesday — four hours**

- 30 minutes: setup and a recorded sound/focus check.
- 90 minutes: record the main performance and essential supporting shots.
- 30 minutes: transfer, organize, and back up footage; verify files open.
- 90 minutes: assemble the complete rough cut. It can be ugly, but the whole argument must exist.

**Wednesday — four hours**

- 2 hours: edit for clarity and pace. Show the strongest evidence early enough to earn the viewer's attention.
- 1 hour: fix sound and color, create the thumbnail, and check captions.
- 1 hour: submit the rough cut for critique and make one targeted career contact. If no human responds in time, do a self-review and proceed.

**Thursday — four hours**

- 90 minutes: make the three highest-priority revisions and export.
- 30 minutes: watch the exported file, correct defects, and publish by 4 p.m.
- 90 minutes: complete two more targeted career actions.
- 30 minutes: log hours, lessons, and next week's brief.

If production overruns, cut optional graphics, extra shots, and runtime first. If a deadline is missed, diagnose the bottleneck and reset the smallest finishable scope. Do not respond by doubling next week's workload.

### Week 2: become dependable on a real-world shoot

Practice a talking-head/interview setup using existing equipment. Aim for separation from the background, a motivated light direction, consistent white balance, and clean recorded speech. Capture five useful supporting shots: wide context, medium action, close detail, hands/process, and reaction/result.

Use an available willing subject with permission, existing authorized footage, or yourself explaining a real process. Do not let finding the perfect subject block delivery. A speculative sample must be clearly labeled; do not imply a client commission or fabricate a testimonial.

Submit a one-page production brief with audience, objective, deliverables, schedule, and approval assumptions. This tests producing as well as shooting.

### Week 3: edit for understanding

Before adding effects, create a three-column map: claim, evidence, visual. Every segment needs a job. Delete repeated explanations. Compare the first 30 seconds of the first and second videos. Ask an outside viewer what they expect after watching the opening and what they actually remember after finishing.

### Week 4: deliver efficiently

Reuse footage legitimately. Make a concise business cut with a clear opening, supporting evidence, and appropriate next action. Practice version naming, caption accuracy, aspect-ratio decisions, and a concise delivery note. Keep the week to eight hours around Thanksgiving. Do not begin a new shoot.

### Week 5: repeat what is working

Choose a related movie topic for the same viewer. Write the title/promise before the script. Change one substantial variable based on feedback, such as a faster arrival at evidence. Record the reason for the change so the result is interpretable. Offer a tightly scoped paid deliverable to one relevant prospect, with the scope, revisions, schedule, and fee clearly stated after estimating effort.

### Week 6: capstone and career decision

Produce the fourth YouTube piece using the established workflow. Select the strongest two portfolio samples, identify the specific role played on each, and prepare a concise comparison of week 1 versus week 6. Do not rebuild the entire portfolio. Review the evidence on December 14.

## How coaching and accountability work

ChatGPT can help plan, review accessible scripts and media, prioritize revisions, and interpret supplied analytics. It cannot autonomously observe work, guarantee professional-level media review for unsupported uploads, secure clients, or enforce a deadline. No reminder or scheduled check-in has been created by this course.

Bring this check-in at the start of each working session:

> Today's deliverable:
> Available time:
> Current version/link or material:
> Biggest blocker:
> What I need reviewed:

For rough-cut review, supply an accessible video or supported upload. If that cannot be inspected, provide the transcript, selected frames, and timecoded notes; feedback will be limited accordingly. Reviews should identify what was actually inspected.

The response should contain: a clear next assignment, the three most consequential fixes, and a realistic stopping point. Feedback should be direct and specific, never punishment for a missed target. A missed week is information about the plan, not a judgment of character.

### Instructor rubric

Score each category from 1 to 5. One means the problem substantially interferes with the video; three means understandable and functional; five means deliberate and polished. Scores are internal progress measures, not industry certification.

| Category | Review question |
|---|---|
| Promise and audience | Is it immediately clear who this is for and why they should care? |
| Story and evidence | Does each segment advance a supported argument or useful story? |
| Sound and image | Can we hear comfortably and see the intended subject clearly? |
| Editing | Does pacing support understanding without unnecessary repetition? |
| Packaging and delivery | Does the title/thumbnail or client brief accurately match the finished work? |

Keep the score out of the creative process until a complete rough cut exists. A publishable video also needs factual accuracy, appropriate permissions, and no major technical defects regardless of score.

## Career development without endless cold outreach

Aim for 16 carefully chosen career actions across six weeks: three in each normal week and one in the holiday week. These are controllable attempts, not promised conversations.

Priority order:

1. Relevant existing contacts or clients where a clear video need exists.
2. Local production companies and agencies that use freelance editors or production support.
3. Specific employed or part-time production/editing roles that fit demonstrated skills.

Each action names the recipient's likely need, links one relevant sample, states Nick's role, and asks for one concrete next step. Do not send a generic portfolio dump. Log date, organization, role/need, sample sent, response, follow-up date, and next action. Do not send unsolicited repeated follow-ups indefinitely.

Target outcomes, not guarantees: at least three substantive industry conversations and one credible paid next step, interview, or trial assignment. A positive reply without a concrete need is encouraging but not income evidence. Do not accept unpaid bespoke client work merely to create the appearance of traction.

## YouTube measurement

Review approximately seven days after each upload when data is available. Do not judge each video by the first hour or compare a tiny channel directly with established creators.

Record: topic, length, title/thumbnail, production hours, views, impressions, traffic sources, click-through rate where available, average view duration, percentage viewed, retention around the opening when available, and specific audience comments.

Use the data to ask questions, not declare causes from tiny samples:

- Impressions with few clicks may suggest the topic or packaging needs attention, but traffic source and audience match matter.
- Early departures may suggest a mismatch between the promise and opening, slow setup, or weak clarity.
- A middle drop can identify a section worth inspecting; it does not prove why people left.
- Low impressions do not establish that the editing is poor.

Do not set a universal click-through or retention threshold as a pass/fail test. Make comparisons cautiously among similar videos and traffic sources. Record when evidence is insufficient. Six weeks can test a sustainable workflow and early audience signals; it cannot reliably establish long-term channel earning potential.

## December 14 decision

Evaluate craft, sustainability, and commercial demand separately.

- **Continue production focus:** the work is sustainable, quality improves, and there are credible professional conversations or paid opportunities. Choose the strongest specialty for the next 90 days.
- **Shift toward an employed role:** making videos is satisfying but freelance selling and administration are not. Use the strongest relevant samples in a focused employment search.
- **Keep YouTube as a bounded creative investment:** the process is enjoyable and audience evidence is promising, while income comes from paid work. Four uploads are too few to declare YouTube a dependable livelihood.
- **Revise the offer or outreach:** quality improves but commercial response is weak. Investigate targeting and offer clarity before assuming more camera practice will solve sales.
- **Test a different career:** the work itself remains consistently unappealing or unsustainable despite a reasonable workload. Arrange a real-world exposure to one alternative before enrolling in retraining.

Six deliverables, clearer skills, and honest evidence are the trial's promised structure. Revenue, hiring, and audience growth remain uncertain outcomes.
